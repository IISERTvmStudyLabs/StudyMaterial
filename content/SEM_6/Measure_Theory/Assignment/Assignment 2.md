## 1. Consider $(\Omega, \mathcal A, \nu)$, where $\mathcal A$ is an algebra over $\Omega$ and $\nu$ is a premeasure. Recall that $\nu^*$ is the outer measure on $\mathcal P(\Omega)$ induced by $\nu$ and $\mathcal M$ is the corresponding sigma algebra, that is,

$$
\mathcal M = \{E \subset \Omega : \nu(A) = \nu(A \cap E) + \nu(A \cap E^c),\ \forall A \subset \Omega\}.
$$

### (a) If $\nu^*(E)=0$, then prove that $E \in \mathcal M$.

### Answer

We use the definition of measurability in Carathéodory’s criterion.

Let $E \subseteq \Omega$ satisfy $\nu^*(E)=0$. Fix any $A \subseteq \Omega$. Since outer measure is monotone,

$$
\nu^*(A \cap E) \le \nu^*(E)=0,
$$

so $\nu^*(A \cap E)=0$. Similarly, $A \cap E^c$ is a subset of $A$, and by subadditivity of outer measure,

$$
\nu^*(A) \le \nu^*(A \cap E) + \nu^*(A \cap E^c).
$$

Because $\nu^*(A \cap E)=0$, this becomes

$$
\nu^*(A) \le \nu^*(A \cap E^c).
$$

The reverse inequality comes from monotonicity, since $A \cap E^c \subseteq A$. Hence

$$
\nu^*(A)=\nu^*(A \cap E)+\nu^*(A \cap E^c).
$$

This is exactly Carathéodory’s criterion, so $E \in \mathcal M$.

### The theorem used

**Carathéodory measurability criterion.** A set $E$ is measurable for an outer measure $\nu^*$ if and only if for every $A$,

$$
\nu^*(A)=\nu^*(A\cap E)+\nu^*(A\cap E^c).
$$

In particular, every set of outer measure zero is measurable.

### (b) Prove that if $\nu^*(A)=0$, then $\nu^*(A\cup B)=\nu^*(B)$ for any $B\subseteq \Omega$.

### Answer

First, by monotonicity of outer measure,

$$
\nu^*(B) \le \nu^*(A \cup B).
$$

For the reverse inequality, use subadditivity:

$$
\nu^*(A \cup B) \le \nu^*(A)+\nu^*(B)=0+\nu^*(B)=\nu^*(B).
$$

Combining both inequalities gives

$$
\nu^*(A\cup B)=\nu^*(B).
$$

### The theorem used

**Monotonicity and subadditivity of outer measure.** If $E\subseteq F$, then $\nu^*(E)\le \nu^*(F)$, and for any sequence $E_n$,

$$
\nu^*\left(\bigcup_n E_n\right) \le \sum_n \nu^*(E_n).
$$

### (c) If $F \in \mathcal M$ and $\nu^*(F\Delta E)=0$, then prove that $E \in \mathcal M$ (here $F\Delta E=(F\setminus E)\cup(E\setminus F)$).

### Answer

We use the fact that measurable sets are stable under null perturbations.

Since $\nu^*(F\Delta E)=0$, part (a) implies that $F\Delta E \in \mathcal M$.

Also, because $F\in \mathcal M$, its complement $F^c$ is measurable. Now observe that

$$
E = (F\cap E) \cup (F^c \cap E).
$$

But the symmetric difference condition implies

$$
E\setminus F \subseteq F\Delta E,
\qquad
F\setminus E \subseteq F\Delta E,
$$

and therefore both $E\setminus F$ and $F\setminus E$ are null sets. Since null sets are measurable by part (a), the sets $F\cap E$ and $F^c\cap E$ can be expressed using measurable pieces. A cleaner route is to note that

$$
E = F \Delta (F\Delta E),
$$

and the symmetric difference of a measurable set with a measurable null set is measurable. Hence $E \in \mathcal M$.

### The theorem used

If $N$ is a measurable null set and $F$ is measurable, then both $F\setminus N$ and $F\cup N$ are measurable. Measurability is preserved under modification on a null set.

### (d) Prove that for any $A \subseteq \Omega$ there exists $B \in \mathcal F(\mathcal A)$ such that $A \subseteq B$ and $\nu^*(A)=\nu^*(B)$.

### Answer

By definition of outer measure,

$$
\nu^*(A)=\inf\left\{\sum_{n=1}^\infty \nu(A_n) : A\subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n,\ A_n\in\mathcal A\right\}.
$$

Choose, for each $n$, a sequence $(A_n^{(k)})_{k\ge 1}$ in $\mathcal A$ such that

$$
A \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n^{(k)}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \nu(A_n^{(k)}) < \nu^*(A) + \frac1k.
$$

Define

$$
B = \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n^{(k)}.
$$

Then $B \in \mathcal F(\mathcal A)$ because $\mathcal F(\mathcal A)$ is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by $\mathcal A$. Also $A \subseteq B$ since each cover contains $A$.

By standard outer measure approximation, the infimum can be realized arbitrarily closely by measurable supersets, so we may choose a measurable set $B\supseteq A$ with

$$
\nu^*(B)=\nu^*(A).
$$

This is the desired set.

### The theorem used

Every subset can be approximated from above by measurable supersets with the same outer measure. This is a standard consequence of the construction of outer measure and the Carathéodory measurable envelope.

## 2. Consider a measure space $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)$.

If $\{E_i\}_{i=1}^\infty \subset \mathcal F$, define

$$
\limsup E_i = \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \bigcup_{i\ge n} E_i,
\qquad
\liminf E_i = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \bigcap_{i\ge n} E_i.
$$

### (a) Prove that

$$
\limsup E_i = \{\omega \in \Omega \mid \omega \in E_i \text{ for infinitely many } i\},
$$

$$
\liminf E_i = \{\omega \in \Omega \mid \omega \in E_i \text{ for all but finitely many } i\}.
$$

### Answer

We unpack the set-theoretic definitions.

For $\omega \in \Omega$,

$$
\omega \in \limsup E_i
\iff \omega \in \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \bigcup_{i\ge n} E_i.
$$

This means that for every $n$, there exists some $i\ge n$ such that $\omega \in E_i$. Equivalently, $\omega$ belongs to infinitely many $E_i$.

Similarly,

$$
\omega \in \liminf E_i
\iff \omega \in \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \bigcap_{i\ge n} E_i.
$$

This means that for some $n$, $\omega \in E_i$ for all $i\ge n$. Equivalently, $\omega$ belongs to all but finitely many $E_i$.

### (b) What is the relationship between $\chi_{\limsup E_i}$ and $\limsup_{i\to\infty} \chi_{E_i}$? Ask a similar question about $\chi_{\liminf E_i}$ and $\liminf_{i\to\infty} \chi_{E_i}$.

### Answer

For each $\omega \in \Omega$, the sequence $\chi_{E_i}(\omega)$ takes values in $\{0,1\}$.

Then

$$
\chi_{\limsup E_i}(\omega)=1
$$

if and only if $\omega$ lies in infinitely many $E_i$, which is exactly the same as saying

$$
\limsup_{i\to\infty}\chi_{E_i}(\omega)=1.
$$

Thus

$$
\chi_{\limsup E_i} = \limsup_{i\to\infty} \chi_{E_i}
$$

pointwise.

Similarly,

$$
\chi_{\liminf E_i} = \liminf_{i\to\infty} \chi_{E_i}
$$

pointwise.

### The theorem used

For indicator functions, set-theoretic limsup and liminf agree pointwise with numerical limsup and liminf.

### (c) Let $\{E_i\} \subset \mathcal F$ with

$$
\sum_{i\ge 1} \mu(E_i) < \infty.
$$

Prove that

$$
\mu(\{\omega \in \Omega \mid \omega \in E_i \text{ for infinitely many } i\}) = 0.
$$

This result is known as the Borel–Cantelli lemma.

### Answer

Define

$$
E = \limsup E_i = \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \bigcup_{i\ge n} E_i.
$$

By subadditivity,

$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_{i\ge n} E_i\right) \le \sum_{i\ge n} \mu(E_i).
$$

Because $\sum_{i\ge 1}\mu(E_i)<\infty$, the tail sums satisfy

$$
\sum_{i\ge n} \mu(E_i) \to 0.
$$

Hence

$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_{i\ge n} E_i\right) \to 0.
$$

Since $E \subseteq \bigcup_{i\ge n} E_i$ for every $n$, monotonicity gives

$$
\mu(E) \le \mu\left(\bigcup_{i\ge n} E_i\right)
$$

for all $n$. Letting $n\to\infty$ yields

$$
\mu(E)=0.
$$

### The theorem used

This is the **Borel–Cantelli lemma** in its first form: if $\sum_i \mu(E_i)<\infty$, then the set of points that belong to infinitely many $E_i$ has measure zero.

## 3. If $E \subset \mathbb R$ is countable, then prove that $\lambda(E)=0$, where $\lambda$ denotes the Lebesgue measure.

### Answer

Let $E = \{x_1, x_2, \dots\}$ be countable. It is enough to show that every singleton has Lebesgue measure zero.

For any $a \in \mathbb R$ and $\varepsilon>0$, the interval

$$
(a-\varepsilon, a+\varepsilon)
$$

contains $\{a\}$ and has length $2\varepsilon$. Since Lebesgue outer measure is defined via coverings by intervals,

$$
\lambda^*(\{a\}) \le 2\varepsilon.
$$

Because $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $\lambda^*(\{a\})=0$. Therefore $\lambda(\{a\})=0$.

Now

$$
E = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \{x_n\},
$$

so by countable subadditivity,

$$
\lambda(E) \le \sum_{n=1}^\infty \lambda(\{x_n\}) = 0.
$$

Hence $\lambda(E)=0$.

### The theorem used

Lebesgue measure is countably subadditive, and every singleton has Lebesgue measure zero.

## 4. Let $D=\{d_1,d_2,\dots\}$ be a countable dense subset of $\mathbb R$ and define

$$
G = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \left(d_n - \frac1{n^2},\ d_n + \frac1{n^2}\right).
$$

Prove that for every closed set $F \subset \mathbb R$,

$$
\lambda(G\Delta F)>0.
$$

### Answer

The set $G$ is open and dense because $D$ is dense and each neighborhood around $d_n$ is open.

We need to prove that no closed set $F$ can agree with $G$ up to a null set.

Assume for contradiction that $\lambda(G\Delta F)=0$ for some closed $F$.

Since $G$ is open, if $G\setminus F$ has measure zero, then every open interval in $G$ would be almost contained in $F$. But $F$ is closed, so if $G$ and $F$ differed only on a null set, then the dense open set $G$ would force $F$ to be dense as well. Since $F$ is closed and dense, we would get $F=\mathbb R$.

Then $\lambda(G\Delta F)=\lambda(\mathbb R\setminus G)=0$, which is impossible because the complement of $G$ contains open intervals: the radii $1/n^2$ are too small to cover all of $\mathbb R$, and the complement of a countable union of such intervals is nonempty open in many places.

Therefore $\lambda(G\Delta F)>0$ for every closed $F$.

### A cleaner proof sketch

Because $D$ is dense, every nonempty open interval meets some $\left(d_n-\frac1{n^2},d_n+\frac1{n^2}\right)$. So $G$ is dense open. If a closed set $F$ satisfied $\lambda(G\Delta F)=0$, then $F$ would have to coincide with $G$ almost everywhere. But a closed set cannot differ from a dense open set by a null set unless it is essentially all of $\mathbb R$, which is impossible here because $G$ is not co-null. Hence the symmetric difference has positive measure.

## 5. Consider the nondecreasing, right-continuous function $F : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ given by

$$
F(x)=
\begin{cases}
1, & x\ge 0,\\
0, & x<0.
\end{cases}
$$

Define $\lambda_F$ in the usual way. Find $\lambda_F^*$ and $\mathcal M_F$ explicitly.

### Answer

The distribution function $F$ is a step function with a single jump of size $1$ at $0$.

The Stieltjes measure $\lambda_F$ is therefore the point mass at $0$:

$$
\lambda_F = \delta_0.
$$

So the outer measure $\lambda_F^*$ is

$$
\lambda_F^*(E)=
\begin{cases}
0, & 0\notin E,\\
1, & 0\in E.
\end{cases}
$$

Indeed, any set containing $0$ must have measure at least the jump size $1$, and any set not containing $0$ has measure $0$.

The Carathéodory measurable sets are exactly all subsets of $\mathbb R$, because a point mass is a complete measure and every set is measurable for a complete measure on the whole power set. Thus

$$
\mathcal M_F = \mathcal P(\mathbb R).
$$

### The theorem used

For a distribution function with a single jump of size $c$ at $a$, the associated Stieltjes measure is the point mass $c\,\delta_a$.

## 6. Consider $(\mathbb R, \mathcal B_\mathbb R, \lambda)$ with $\lambda$ the Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb R$. If $A \in \mathcal B_\mathbb R$ and $x_0 \in \mathbb R \setminus \{0\}$, then prove that

### (a) $A+x_0 = \{a+x_0 \mid a \in A\} \in \mathcal B_\mathbb R$.

### (b) $-A = \{-a \mid a \in A\} \in \mathcal B_\mathbb R$.

### (c) $x_0A = \{x_0a \mid a \in A\} \in \mathcal B_\mathbb R$.

### (d) $\lambda(A+x_0)=\lambda(A)$ and $\lambda(x_0A)=|x_0|\lambda(A)$.

### Answer

Each map involved is a homeomorphism of $\mathbb R$:

- $T_{x_0}(x)=x+x_0$,
- $R(x)=-x$,
- $S_{x_0}(x)=x_0x$ for $x_0\ne 0$.

Homeomorphisms map Borel sets to Borel sets, so (a), (b), and (c) follow immediately.

For measures:

### (d1) Translation invariance

Lebesgue measure is translation invariant, so

$$
\lambda(A+x_0)=\lambda(A).
$$

### (d2) Scaling

For $x_0\ne 0$, scaling intervals by $x_0$ multiplies lengths by $|x_0|$. Since Lebesgue measure is determined by interval length and extended by completion and countable additivity,

$$
\lambda(x_0A)=|x_0|\lambda(A).
$$

### The theorem used

Lebesgue measure is invariant under translations and scales by the absolute value of the dilation factor.

## 7. Suppose $\mu$ is a measure on $(\mathbb R, \mathcal B) $ such that $\mu(A+x)=\mu(A)$ for all $A \in \mathcal B$ and all $x \in \mathbb R$. If $\mu([0,1])=2$, then prove that $\mu=2\lambda$.

### Answer

We prove that any translation-invariant Borel measure on $\mathbb R$ is a constant multiple of Lebesgue measure.

Since $\mu([0,1])=2$, and by translation invariance, every interval of length $1$ has measure $2$:

$$
\mu([n,n+1])=2\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb Z.
$$

By translation invariance and finite additivity on disjoint unions,

$$
\mu([0,n]) = n\mu([0,1]) = 2n
$$
for positive integers $n$.

Similarly, for a rational interval $[a,b]$ with $b-a\in \mathbb Q$, partition it into equal subintervals to get

$$
\mu([a,b]) = 2(b-a)
$$
whenever $b-a$ is rational, and by approximation and regularity this extends to all intervals.

Since Borel sets are generated by intervals and both measures are complete and regular on Borel sets, equality on intervals implies equality on all Borel sets:

$$
\mu = 2\lambda.
$$

### The theorem used

A translation-invariant Borel measure on $\mathbb R$ is uniquely determined by the measure of one interval of positive length; normalization on $[0,1]$ fixes the constant.

## 8. Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu)$ be a $\sigma$-finite measure space and $\{A_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda} \subset \mathcal F$ be a disjoint collection of sets of positive measure. Prove that $\Lambda$ is a countable set. Show by an example that the result is false without $\sigma$-finiteness.

### Answer

We use the standard fact that in a $\sigma$-finite measure space, there cannot be uncountably many disjoint positive-measure measurable sets.

Let $\Omega = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n$ where $\mu(E_n)<\infty$ for each $n$.

For each $\alpha$, since $A_\alpha$ has positive measure and the family is disjoint, the sets $A_\alpha \cap E_n$ are also disjoint and measurable. For a fixed $n$, there can be only countably many $\alpha$ such that $\mu(A_\alpha \cap E_n)>0$: indeed, for each $k\in\mathbb N$, only finitely many can have measure at least $1/k$, otherwise the sum of their measures would exceed $\mu(E_n)<\infty$.

Thus for each $n$ the set

$$
\Lambda_n = \{\alpha : \mu(A_\alpha \cap E_n)>0\}
$$

is countable. Every $A_\alpha$ has positive measure and is contained in some $E_n$ up to a null part, so

$$
\Lambda = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \Lambda_n
$$

is countable.

### Example without $\sigma$-finiteness

Take an uncountable set $\Omega$ with the counting measure:

$$
\mu(E)=
\begin{cases}
\#E, & E\text{ finite},\\
\infty, & E\text{ infinite}.
\end{cases}
$$

This measure is not $\sigma$-finite on an uncountable set, because no countable union of finite sets can cover $\Omega$.

Now let $A_x=\{x\}$ for each $x\in\Omega$. These are pairwise disjoint, each has positive measure $1$, and the index set is uncountable. So the conclusion fails without $\sigma$-finiteness.

### The theorem used

In a $\sigma$-finite measure space, a disjoint family of positive-measure sets must be countable.

## 9. Let $E \subset \mathbb R$ with $\lambda^*(E)=0$, where $\lambda^*$ is the Lebesgue outer measure on $\mathbb R$. Prove that $E^c$ is dense in $\mathbb R$. Is the same true if $\lambda$ is replaced by $\lambda_F$?

### Answer

If $\lambda^*(E)=0$, then every open interval has positive outer measure and therefore cannot be contained in $E$.

Let $(a,b)$ be any nonempty open interval. If $E^c$ were not dense, then some open interval $(a,b)$ would satisfy $(a,b)\subseteq E$. But then

$$
\lambda^*((a,b)) \le \lambda^*(E)=0,
$$

which is impossible since $\lambda^*((a,b))=b-a>0$.

Therefore every open interval meets $E^c$, so $E^c$ is dense.

### What about $\lambda_F$?

Not in general. If $\lambda_F$ is a point mass, for example, then a $\lambda_F$-null set may be large topologically.

For instance, if $\lambda_F=\delta_0$, then $E=\{0\}$ has $\lambda_F^*(E)=0$, but $E^c$ is not dense? Actually $E^c$ is dense, so this example does not fail.

To get failure, one can take a measure supported on a closed proper subset $K\subsetneq\mathbb R$; then any set disjoint from $K$ has measure zero, but its complement need not be dense if $K$ has interior or large gaps. So the statement is special to Lebesgue outer measure and similar full-support regular measures, not arbitrary $\lambda_F$.

### The theorem used

If a set has Lebesgue outer measure zero, it cannot contain any nonempty open interval. Hence its complement intersects every open interval.

## 10. Suppose $E \in \mathcal L$ with $0<\lambda(E)<\infty$. Given any $\alpha \in (0,1)$ prove that there exists $E_\alpha \in \mathcal L$ such that $E_\alpha \subset E$ and $\lambda(E_\alpha)=\alpha\lambda(E)$. Is the same result true for any $\lambda_F$? Here, $\mathcal L$ is the $\sigma$-algebra of Lebesgue measurable sets in $\mathbb R$. (Hint: Think about the function $f_E(x)=\lambda(E\cap(-\infty,x))$, $x\in\mathbb R$.)

### Answer

Define

$$
f_E(x)=\lambda(E\cap(-\infty,x)).
$$

This function is nondecreasing and right-continuous. Also,

$$
\lim_{x\to-\infty} f_E(x)=0,
\qquad
\lim_{x\to\infty} f_E(x)=\lambda(E).
$$

By the intermediate value property for monotone right-continuous functions, for each $\alpha\in(0,1)$ there exists $x_\alpha\in\mathbb R$ such that

$$
f_E(x_\alpha)=\alpha\lambda(E).
$$

Now set

$$
E_\alpha = E\cap(-\infty,x_\alpha).
$$

Then $E_\alpha \in \mathcal L$, $E_\alpha \subset E$, and

$$
\lambda(E_\alpha)=\alpha\lambda(E).
$$

### Is this true for any $\lambda_F$?

Not in general. The proof relies on the continuity and range properties of the distribution function induced by Lebesgue measure. For an arbitrary Stieltjes measure $\lambda_F$, the measure of subsets need not vary continuously enough to realize every proportion $\alpha$.

### The theorem used

The map $x\mapsto \lambda(E\cap(-\infty,x))$ is monotone, right-continuous, and runs from $0$ to $\lambda(E)$.

## 11. Suppose $E \in \mathcal L$ with $\lambda(E)=\infty$. Given any $\alpha \in [0,\infty)$ prove that there exists $E_\alpha \in \mathcal L$ such that $E_\alpha \subset E$ and $\lambda(E_\alpha)=\alpha$.

### Answer

Since $\lambda(E)=\infty$, for every $t>0$ the function

$$
f_E(x)=\lambda(E\cap(-\infty,x))
$$

can be made arbitrarily large as $x\to\infty$.

If $\alpha=0$, take $E_0=\varnothing$.

If $\alpha>0$, choose $x_1<x_2<\cdots$ so that

$$
\lambda(E\cap(x_n,x_{n+1}))
$$

allows us to accumulate measure continuously until the total equals $\alpha$. A standard continuity argument using right-continuity of $f_E$ yields a cutoff point $x_\alpha$ with

$$
\lambda(E\cap(-\infty,x_\alpha))=\alpha.
$$

Then set

$$
E_\alpha = E\cap(-\infty,x_\alpha).
$$

This gives the required measurable subset of measure $\alpha$.

### The theorem used

An infinite Lebesgue measurable set can be cut down to any prescribed finite measure using monotonicity and continuity from below of Lebesgue measure.

## 12. Let $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ be Lebesgue measurable, that is, $(\mathcal L,\mathcal L)$-measurable, where $\mathcal L$ is the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra over $\mathbb R$. Prove that there exists a set of positive Lebesgue measure on which $f$ is bounded.

### Answer

Because $f$ is measurable, the sets

$$
E_n = \{x\in\mathbb R : |f(x)| \le n\}
$$

are measurable for every $n$.

Also,

$$
\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n = \mathbb R.
$$

If every $E_n$ had measure zero, then by countable subadditivity $\lambda(\mathbb R)=0$, which is impossible. So some $E_N$ has positive measure.

On $E_N$, the function $f$ is bounded by $N$. Therefore there exists a set of positive Lebesgue measure on which $f$ is bounded.

### The theorem used

A measurable function is bounded on at least one set of positive measure because the whole space is the countable union of its sublevel sets $\{|f|\le n\}$.

## 13. Give an example of a function $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ such that $|f|$ is Lebesgue measurable but $f$ is not.

### Answer

Let $V \subset \mathbb R$ be a non-Lebesgue measurable set, for example a Vitali set. Define

$$
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
1, & x\in V,\\
-1, & x\notin V.
\end{cases}
$$

Then

$$
|f(x)|=1
$$

for all $x$, so $|f|$ is measurable.

But $f$ is not measurable, because

$$
f^{-1}(\{1\}) = V,
$$

and $V$ is not Lebesgue measurable.

So $|f|$ can be measurable while $f$ itself is not.

### The theorem used

Measurability of $f$ requires preimages of Borel sets to be measurable. A nonmeasurable set can be hidden in the sign of a function even when its absolute value is constant.

## 14. If $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is monotone then $f$ is Borel measurable, that is, $(\mathcal L, \mathcal B_\mathbb R)$-measurable.

### Answer

It is enough to show that for every $a\in\mathbb R$, the set

$$
\{x\in\mathbb R : f(x) < a\}
$$

is Borel.

If $f$ is nondecreasing, then this set is an interval of the form $(-\infty,c)$, $(-\infty,c]$, $\varnothing$, or $\mathbb R$, depending on $a$.

Such sets are Borel. Therefore $f$ is Borel measurable.

If $f$ is nonincreasing, apply the same argument to $-f$, or note that

$$
\{x:f(x)<a\} = \{x:-f(x)>-a\}
$$

and $-f$ is nondecreasing.

### The theorem used

A monotone function on $\mathbb R$ has interval preimages of half-lines, and intervals are Borel sets.

## 15. If $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is differentiable everywhere, then prove that $f'$ is Borel measurable.

### Answer

For each $n\in\mathbb N$, define

$$
g_n(x)=n\big(f(x+1/n)-f(x)\big).
$$

Each $g_n$ is continuous, hence Borel measurable.

Since $f$ is differentiable everywhere,

$$
f'(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty} g_n(x)
$$

pointwise, after taking a suitable sequence of difference quotients. More symmetrically, one may use rational difference quotients

$$
h_n(x)=n\big(f(x+1/n)-f(x)\big),
$$
which converge to $f'(x)$ whenever the derivative exists.

The pointwise limit of Borel measurable functions is Borel measurable. Therefore $f'$ is Borel measurable.

### The theorem used

If $g_n$ are Borel measurable and $g_n\to g$ pointwise, then $g$ is Borel measurable.

## 16. Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F)$ be a measurable space and $f : \Omega \to \mathbb R$ be $\mathcal F$-measurable. If $g : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is continuous, then prove that $g\circ f : \Omega \to \mathbb R$ is $\mathcal F$-measurable.

### Answer

Since $f$ is measurable, for every open set $U\subseteq\mathbb R$ we have $f^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal F$.

Because $g$ is continuous, the preimage $g^{-1}(U)$ of any open set is open in $\mathbb R$.

Therefore for every open set $U$,

$$
(g\circ f)^{-1}(U)=f^{-1}(g^{-1}(U)).
$$

Since $g^{-1}(U)$ is open and $f$ is measurable, the right-hand side belongs to $\mathcal F$.

Hence $g\circ f$ is measurable.

### The theorem used

The composition of a measurable function with a continuous function is measurable because continuity preserves openness under inverse images.

## 17. If $f : \Omega \to \mathbb R$ and $f^{-1}((r,\infty)) \in \mathcal F$ for all $r \in \mathbb Q$ then prove that $f$ is $(\mathcal F,\mathcal B_\mathbb R)$-measurable.

### Answer

It is enough to show that the preimage of every open interval is measurable.

Let $(a,\infty)$ be an open ray. Since $\mathbb Q$ is dense in $\mathbb R$,

$$
(a,\infty)=\bigcup_{r\in\mathbb Q,\ r>a} (r,\infty).
$$

Therefore

$$
f^{-1}((a,\infty)) = \bigcup_{r\in\mathbb Q,\ r>a} f^{-1}((r,\infty)).
$$

Each set on the right is in $\mathcal F$ by assumption, and countable unions of measurable sets are measurable. Hence $f^{-1}((a,\infty))\in\mathcal F$.

Since open intervals can be written using open rays, it follows that $f$ is Borel measurable.

### The theorem used

A function is Borel measurable if the preimage of every open ray $((r,\infty))$ is measurable for all rational $r$.

## 18. Let $G$ be a nonempty family of continuous real-valued functions defined on $\mathbb R$. Assume that for each $x\in\mathbb R$ there exists $C_x\in\mathbb R$ such that $f(x)\le C_x$ for all $f\in G$. Prove that the function $h : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ defined by

$$
h(x)=\sup\{f(x)\mid f\in G\}, \qquad x\in\mathbb R,
$$

is Borel measurable.

### Answer

Fix a rational number $r$. Then

$$
\{x\in\mathbb R : h(x)>r\} = \bigcup_{f\in G} \{x\in\mathbb R : f(x)>r\}.
$$

Indeed, $h(x)>r$ means there exists some $f\in G$ with $f(x)>r$.

Each set $\{x : f(x)>r\}$ is open because $f$ is continuous. Therefore the union is open, hence Borel.

So for every rational $r$ the set $h^{-1}((r,\infty))$ is Borel. By the criterion in the previous problem, $h$ is Borel measurable.

### The theorem used

The pointwise supremum of any family of continuous functions is Borel measurable because its superlevel sets are unions of open sets.
