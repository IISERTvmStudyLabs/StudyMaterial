## Question 1

Let
$$
\mathcal L=\{[a,b): a,b\in\mathbb R,\ a<b\}
$$
be the collection of all semi-open intervals in $\mathbb R$.

### (a) Prove that $\mathcal L$ is a basis for a topology on $\mathbb R$.

### Solution

To show that $\mathcal L$ is a basis, we verify the two basis axioms.

#### Axiom 1: Covering

For every $x\in\mathbb R$, the interval
$$
[x,x+1)
$$
belongs to $\mathcal L$ and contains $x$. Hence every point of $\mathbb R$ lies in some element of $\mathcal L$.

#### Axiom 2: Refinement under intersections

Let
$$
[a,b),\ [c,d)\in\mathcal L
$$
and let $x\in[a,b)\cap[c,d)$.
Then
$$
x\ge \max\{a,c\}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
x<\min\{b,d\}.
$$
So
$$
[\max\{a,c\},\min\{b,d\})
$$
is a semi-open interval containing $x$ and contained in the intersection. If the intersection is empty, there is nothing to prove.

Thus $\mathcal L$ is a basis for a topology on $\mathbb R$.

So
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L\text{ is a basis for a topology on }\mathbb R.}
$$

---

### (b) Compare the topologies $\mathcal T_\mathcal L$ and $\mathcal T$.

Here $\mathcal T_\mathcal L$ denotes the topology generated by $\mathcal L$, and $\mathcal T$ denotes the standard topology on $\mathbb R$.

### Solution

The topology $\mathcal T_\mathcal L$ is strictly finer than the standard topology.

Indeed, every standard open interval $(a,b)$ can be written as
$$
(a,b)=\bigcup_{x\in(a,b)}[x,b),
$$
and each $[x,b)$ belongs to $\mathcal L$. Therefore every standard open set is open in $\mathcal T_\mathcal L$.

So
$$
\mathcal T\subseteq \mathcal T_\mathcal L.
$$

The inclusion is strict because $[0,1)$ belongs to $\mathcal T_\mathcal L$ but is not open in the standard topology.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T\subsetneq \mathcal T_\mathcal L.}
$$

---

Let
$$
\mathcal L_\mathbb Q=\{[p,q): p,q\in\mathbb Q,\ p<q\}.
$$

### (c) Is $\mathcal L_\mathbb Q$ a basis for $\mathcal T_\mathcal L$? Justify.

### Solution

No, $\mathcal L_\mathbb Q$ is **not** a basis for $\mathcal T_\mathcal L$.

To be a basis for $\mathcal T_\mathcal L$, every set in $\mathcal L$ would have to be expressible as a union of elements of $\mathcal L_\mathbb Q$.

Consider the basic open set
$$
[\sqrt2,\sqrt2+1)\in\mathcal L.
$$
If $\mathcal L_\mathbb Q$ were a basis, then for the point $\sqrt2\in[\sqrt2,\sqrt2+1)$ there would exist a rational semi-open interval
$$
[p,q)\in\mathcal L_\mathbb Q
$$
such that
$$
\sqrt2\in[p,q)\subseteq[\sqrt2,\sqrt2+1).
$$
But if $\sqrt2\in[p,q)$, then $p\le \sqrt2$. Since $p\in\mathbb Q$ and $\sqrt2$ is irrational, this forces $p<\sqrt2$. Then the interval $[p,q)$ contains points smaller than $\sqrt2$, so it cannot be contained in $[\sqrt2,\sqrt2+1)$.

This contradiction shows that such a basis does not exist.

Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L_\mathbb Q\text{ is not a basis for }\mathcal T_\mathcal L.}
$$

---

### (d) Is $(\mathbb R,\mathcal T_\mathcal L)$ connected? Justify.

### Solution

The topology generated by $\mathcal L$ is the lower limit topology, also called the Sorgenfrey topology.

Consider the two subsets
$$
(-\infty,0)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
[0,\infty).
$$
Both are open in $\mathcal T_\mathcal L$:

- for $(-\infty,0)$, we have
$$
(-\infty,0)=\bigcup_{x<0}[x,0),
$$
	a union of basic open sets;
- for $[0,\infty)$, we have
$$
[0,\infty)=\bigcup_{n\ge 0}[n,n+1),
$$
	again a union of basic open sets.

They are nonempty, disjoint, and their union is all of $\mathbb R$:
$$
\mathbb R=(-\infty,0)\cup[0,\infty).
$$

Thus $\mathbb R$ is separated into two disjoint nonempty open sets, so it is disconnected.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{(\mathbb R,\mathcal T_\mathcal L)\text{ is not connected}.}
$$

## Question 2

Let $(X,\mathcal T_X)$ and $(Y,\mathcal T_Y)$ be topological spaces and $f:X\to Y$. Suppose $A\subseteq X$.

### (a) When is $f$ said to be continuous?

### Solution

The map $f:X\to Y$ is continuous if and only if for every open set $U\in\mathcal T_Y$, the preimage $f^{-1}(U)$ is open in $X$; that is,
$$
U\in\mathcal T_Y\implies f^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal T_X.
$$

So
$$
\boxed{f\text{ is continuous if preimages of open sets are open}.}
$$

---

### (b) If $f:X\to Y$ is continuous, is the restriction $f|_A:A\to Y$ continuous? Justify.

### Solution

Yes, the restriction is continuous.

Let $U\subseteq Y$ be open. Then
$$
(f|_A)^{-1}(U)=A\cap f^{-1}(U).
$$
Since $f$ is continuous, $f^{-1}(U)$ is open in $X$, and hence $A\cap f^{-1}(U)$ is open in the subspace topology on $A$.

Therefore $f|_A$ is continuous.

So
$$
\boxed{f|_A\text{ is continuous}.}
$$

---

Suppose $X=\mathbb R$ with the standard topology. Let $g,h:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be defined by
$$
g(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,& x\le 0,\\
1,& x>0,
\end{cases}
\qquad
h(x)=
\begin{cases}
x,& x\le 0,\\
x^2,& x>0.
\end{cases}
$$

### (c) Is $g|_{(-\infty,0]}$ continuous? Justify.

### Solution

Yes. On the subspace $(-\infty,0]$, the function $g$ is identically $0$.

Indeed, for every $x\in(-\infty,0]$ we have $g(x)=0$. A constant map is continuous.

Thus
$$
\boxed{g|_{(-\infty,0]}\text{ is continuous}.}
$$

---

### (d) Is $g|_{(0,\infty)}$ continuous? Justify.

### Solution

Yes. On the subspace $(0,\infty)$, the function $g$ is identically $1$.

So it is a constant map, hence continuous.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{g|_{(0,\infty)}\text{ is continuous}.}
$$

---

### (e) Is $g$ continuous on $\mathbb R$? Justify.

### Solution

No. The function has a jump discontinuity at $x=0$.

Indeed,
$$
\lim_{x\to 0^-}g(x)=0,
\qquad
\lim_{x\to 0^+}g(x)=1,
$$
so the left and right limits are different. Therefore $g$ is not continuous at $0$.

Hence
$$
\boxed{g\text{ is not continuous on }\mathbb R.}
$$

---

### (f) Is $h|_{(-\infty,0]}$ continuous? Justify.

### Solution

Yes. On $(-\infty,0]$, the function $h(x)=x$.

The identity function on a subspace of $\mathbb R$ is continuous, so the restriction is continuous.

Thus
$$
\boxed{h|_{(-\infty,0]}\text{ is continuous}.}
$$

---

### (g) Is $h|_{(0,\infty)}$ continuous? Justify.

### Solution

Yes. On $(0,\infty)$, the function $h(x)=x^2$.

Since polynomial functions are continuous, the restriction is continuous.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{h|_{(0,\infty)}\text{ is continuous}.}
$$

---

### (h) Is $h$ continuous on $\mathbb R$? Justify.

### Solution

Yes. We check continuity at the only potentially problematic point, $x=0$.

For $x\le 0$, $h(x)=x$, so
$$
\lim_{x\to 0^-}h(x)=0.
$$
For $x>0$, $h(x)=x^2$, so
$$
\lim_{x\to 0^+}h(x)=0.
$$
Also,
$$
h(0)=0.
$$
Thus the left limit, right limit, and function value agree at $0$. Since $h$ is continuous on each side of $0$ and at $0$, it is continuous on all of $\mathbb R$.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{h\text{ is continuous on }\mathbb R.}
$$

## Question 3

Consider $\mathbb R$ equipped with the Euclidean metric $d$ and the discrete metric $\Delta$, given by
$$
d(x,y)=|x-y|,
\qquad
\Delta(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
0,& x=y,\\
1,& x\ne y.
\end{cases}
$$
Let
$$
I:(\mathbb R,\Delta)\to(\mathbb R,d)
$$
be the identity map $I(x)=x$. Verify whether $I$ is a homeomorphism.

### Solution

We check whether $I$ is continuous and whether its inverse is continuous.

### Step 1: Continuity of $I:(\mathbb R,\Delta)\to(\mathbb R,d)$

Because the domain has the discrete metric, every subset of $\mathbb R$ is open in $(\mathbb R,\Delta)$.
Therefore the preimage under $I$ of any open set in $(\mathbb R,d)$ is open.

Hence $I$ is continuous.

### Step 2: Continuity of the inverse map

The inverse map is again the identity,
$$
I^{-1}:(\mathbb R,d)\to(\mathbb R,\Delta),
\qquad x\mapsto x.
$$
To be continuous, the preimage of every open set in the discrete topology would have to be open in the Euclidean topology.

But in the discrete topology, every singleton $\{a\}$ is open. Its preimage under $I^{-1}$ is just $\{a\}$, and a singleton is not open in the Euclidean topology.

Thus $I^{-1}$ is not continuous.

Therefore $I$ is not a homeomorphism.

So the conclusion is
$$
\boxed{I:(\mathbb R,\Delta)\to(\mathbb R,d)\text{ is continuous but not a homeomorphism}.}
$$

## Question 4

Define the spaces $Y,Y^*\subseteq\mathbb R^2$ as
$$
Y=([0,1]\times\{0\})\cup(C\times[0,1]),
\qquad
C=\left\{\frac1n:n\in\mathbb Z_+\right\},
$$
and
$$
Y^*=(Y\cup(\{0\}\times[0,1]))\setminus\{(0,0)\}.
$$
Prove the following statements.

### (a) $Y$ is path connected.

### Solution

The set $Y$ consists of the horizontal segment $[0,1]\times\{0\}$ together with the vertical segments $\{1/n\}\times[0,1]$ for $n\ge 1$.

To show path connectedness, we exhibit paths joining any point of $Y$ to the point $(1,0)$.

Take any point $(x,y)\in Y$.

- If $(x,y)\in[0,1]\times\{0\}$, then the straight line path
	$$
	\gamma(t)=((1-t)x+t,0),\qquad t\in[0,1],
	$$
	lies entirely in $[0,1]\times\{0\}\subseteq Y$ and joins $(x,0)$ to $(1,0)$.

- If $(x,y)=(1/n,s)$ for some $n\ge 1$ and $s\in[0,1]$, then first move vertically to $(1/n,0)$ and then horizontally to $(1,0)$. This is given by the path
	$$
	\gamma(t)=
	\begin{cases}
	(1/n,(1-2t)s+2ts_0),& 0\le t\le 1/2,\\
	((2t-1)(1-1/n)+1/n,0),& 1/2\le t\le 1,
	\end{cases}
	$$
	where $s_0=0$.

This path lies in $Y$ because the first half stays on the vertical segment $\{1/n\}\times[0,1]$ and the second half stays on the horizontal segment $[0,1]\times\{0\}$.

Thus every point of $Y$ can be joined by a path to $(1,0)$, so any two points of $Y$ can be joined by concatenating their paths to $(1,0)$.

Therefore $Y$ is path connected.

So
$$
\boxed{Y\text{ is path connected}.}
$$

---

### (b) $Y^*$ is connected.

### Solution

The space $Y$ is path connected by part (a), hence connected.

Now observe that every point of $Y^*$ is a limit point of $Y$ in the subspace $Y^*$. Indeed:

- points already in $Y$ are obviously in the closure of $Y$;
- if $(0,t)\in \{0\}\times(0,1]$, then for any neighborhood of $(0,t)$ in $Y^*$, points $\left(\frac1n,t\right)\in Y$ lie in that neighborhood for all sufficiently large $n$.

Therefore $Y$ is dense in $Y^*$, so
$$
\overline{Y}^{\,Y^*}=Y^*.
$$

The closure of a connected set is connected. Since $Y$ is connected, its closure in $Y^*$ is connected. Hence $Y^*$ is connected.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{Y^*\text{ is connected}.}
$$

---

### (c) $Y^*$ is not path connected.

### Solution

We prove that there is no path in $Y^*$ from the point $(0,1)$ to any point of the horizontal base $[0,1]\times\{0\}$.

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a path
$$
\gamma:[0,1]\to Y^*
$$
such that $\gamma(0)=(0,1)$ and $\gamma(1)\in[0,1]\times\{0\}$.

Write $\gamma(t)=(u(t),v(t))$, where $u,v:[0,1]\to\mathbb R$ are continuous.

Let
$$
t_0=\inf\{t\in[0,1]:v(t)=0\}.
$$
Then $v(t)>0$ for all $t<t_0$, and $v(t_0)=0$ by continuity.

For $t<t_0$, the point $\gamma(t)$ lies in the part of $Y^*$ with positive second coordinate. Therefore
$$
u(t)\in C\cup\{0\}.
$$
Since $[0,t_0)$ is connected and $u([0,t_0))\subseteq C\cup\{0\}$, the image $u([0,t_0))$ is a connected subset of $\mathbb R$ contained in the set
$$
C\cup\{0\}=\left\{0,\frac11,\frac12,\frac13,\dots\right\}.
$$
But any connected subset of this set is a singleton, because it contains no interval of positive length.

Hence $u$ is constant on $[0,t_0)$.
Since $u(0)=0$, we get
$$
u(t)=0\qquad\text{for all }t<t_0.
$$
By continuity, $u(t_0)=0$ as well. Therefore
$$
\gamma(t_0)=(0,0),
$$
which is impossible because $(0,0)\notin Y^*$.

This contradiction shows that no such path exists.

Therefore $Y^*$ is not path connected.

So
$$
\boxed{Y^*\text{ is not path connected}.}
$$

---

### (d) $Y^*\cap(\mathbb R\times\{1\})$ is totally disconnected.

### Solution

First identify the set. The intersection with the horizontal line $\mathbb R\times\{1\}$ is
$$
\{(0,1)\}\cup\{(1/n,1): n\in\mathbb Z_+\}.
$$

Consider the projection onto the first coordinate,
$$
\pi_1:Y^*\cap(\mathbb R\times\{1\})\to \mathbb R,
\qquad
\pi_1(x,1)=x.
$$
This is a homeomorphism onto the subset
$$
E=\{0\}\cup\left\{\frac1n:n\in\mathbb Z_+\right\}\subseteq\mathbb R.
$$

Now let $A\subseteq E$ be connected. Since the inclusion map $A\hookrightarrow\mathbb R$ is continuous, $A$ is connected as a subset of $\mathbb R$. But the connected subsets of $\mathbb R$ are intervals, and $E$ contains no nontrivial interval. Hence every connected subset of $E$ is a singleton.

Therefore the connected components of $E$, and hence of $Y^*\cap(\mathbb R\times\{1\})$, are singletons.

Thus the space is totally disconnected.

Hence
$$
\boxed{Y^*\cap(\mathbb R\times\{1\})\text{ is totally disconnected}.}
$$

## Question 5

Let $(Z,d)$ be a metric space and $B\subseteq Z$. For any $z\in Z$, define
$$
d_B(z)=\inf\{d(z,b): b\in B\}.
$$

### (a) Prove that
$$
|d_B(z_1)-d_B(z_2)|\le d(z_1,z_2),\qquad \forall z_1,z_2\in Z.
$$

### Solution

Fix $z_1,z_2\in Z$.
For any $b\in B$, the triangle inequality gives
$$
d(z_1,b)\le d(z_1,z_2)+d(z_2,b).
$$
Taking the infimum over all $b\in B$ on the right-hand side yields
$$
d_B(z_1)\le d(z_1,z_2)+d_B(z_2).
$$
Interchanging the roles of $z_1$ and $z_2$ gives
$$
d_B(z_2)\le d(z_1,z_2)+d_B(z_1).
$$
Combining the two inequalities, we obtain
$$
|d_B(z_1)-d_B(z_2)|\le d(z_1,z_2).
$$

Thus
$$
\boxed{|d_B(z_1)-d_B(z_2)|\le d(z_1,z_2).}
$$

---

Fix $\delta>0$ and define
$$
K_\delta=\{z\in Z: d_B(z)\ge\delta\}.
$$

### (b) Is the set $K_\delta$ open or closed in $Z$? Justify.

### Solution

The function $d_B:Z\to\mathbb R$ is continuous by part (a), because it is $1$-Lipschitz:
$$
|d_B(z_1)-d_B(z_2)|\le d(z_1,z_2).
$$

Now
$$
K_\delta=d_B^{-1}([\delta,\infty)).
$$
Since $[\delta,\infty)$ is closed in $\mathbb R$, its preimage under a continuous map is closed in $Z$.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{K_\delta\text{ is closed in }Z.}
$$

It need not be open in general.

---

### (c) Find $\lim_{\delta\to 0}K_\delta$.

### Solution

As $\delta\downarrow 0$, the sets $K_\delta$ increase:
$$
\delta_1<\delta_2\implies K_{\delta_2}\subseteq K_{\delta_1}.
$$
Hence the natural set-theoretic limit is the union over all positive $\delta$:
$$
\lim_{\delta\to 0^+}K_\delta=\bigcup_{\delta>0}K_\delta.
$$

Now $z\in\bigcup_{\delta>0}K_\delta$ if and only if $d_B(z)\ge\delta$ for some $\delta>0$, which is equivalent to
$$
d_B(z)>0.
$$

Therefore
$$
\lim_{\delta\to 0^+}K_\delta=\{z\in Z: d_B(z)>0\}=Z\setminus\overline{B}.
$$

Indeed, $d_B(z)=0$ exactly when $z$ lies in the closure of $B$.

So
$$
\boxed{\lim_{\delta\to 0^+}K_\delta=Z\setminus\overline{B}.}
$$
