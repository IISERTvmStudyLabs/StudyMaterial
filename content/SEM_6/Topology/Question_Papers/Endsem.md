## Part B

### 9.
Show that
$$
\bigcap_{n\ge 1}\left(-\frac1n,\frac1n\right)
$$
is not an open set in $\mathbb R$ with respect to the finite complement topology.

### Solution

First compute the intersection:
$$
\bigcap_{n\ge 1}\left(-\frac1n,\frac1n\right)=\{0\}.
$$

In the finite complement topology on $\mathbb R$, the open sets are exactly the empty set and the complements of finite subsets of $\mathbb R$.

The set $\{0\}$ is neither empty nor the complement of a finite set. Therefore it is not open in the finite complement topology.

So
$$
\boxed{\bigcap_{n\ge 1}\left(-\frac1n,\frac1n\right)=\{0\}\text{ is not open in the finite complement topology.}}
$$

### 10.
Let $X$ be a set and $\mathcal T_k$ for $k\ge 1$ be a countable collection of topologies on $X$. Then
$$
\mathcal T=\bigcap_{k\ge 1}\mathcal T_k
$$
is also a topology on $X$.

### Solution

We verify the topology axioms.

1. Since each $\mathcal T_k$ is a topology, both $\varnothing$ and $X$ lie in every $\mathcal T_k$. Hence
$$
\varnothing,X\in \bigcap_{k\ge 1}\mathcal T_k=\mathcal T.
$$

2. Let $\{U_i\}_{i\in I}$ be a family of sets in $\mathcal T$. Then each $U_i$ lies in every $\mathcal T_k$. Because each $\mathcal T_k$ is closed under arbitrary unions,
$$
\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i\in \mathcal T_k
$$
for every $k$. Therefore
$$
\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i\in\mathcal T.
$$

3. Let $U,V\in\mathcal T$. Then $U,V\in\mathcal T_k$ for every $k$, and since each $\mathcal T_k$ is closed under finite intersections,
$$
U\cap V\in\mathcal T_k
$$
for every $k$. Hence $U\cap V\in\mathcal T$.

Thus $\mathcal T$ is a topology on $X$.

So
$$
\boxed{\bigcap_{k\ge 1}\mathcal T_k\text{ is a topology on }X.}
$$

### 11.
The identity map
$$
\operatorname{id}:(\mathbb R,\mathcal T_t)\to(\mathbb R,\mathcal T_s)
$$
is continuous, where $\mathcal T_t$ and $\mathcal T_s$ denote the trivial topology and the standard topology respectively.

### Solution

This statement is **false**.

To be continuous, the identity map would have to satisfy that for every open set $U$ in $(\mathbb R,\mathcal T_s)$, the preimage $\operatorname{id}^{-1}(U)=U$ is open in $(\mathbb R,\mathcal T_t)$.

But the trivial topology $\mathcal T_t$ has only two open sets: $\varnothing$ and $\mathbb R$. Since $(0,1)$ is open in the standard topology but not open in the trivial topology, the identity map is not continuous.

So the correct conclusion is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{id}:(\mathbb R,\mathcal T_t)\to(\mathbb R,\mathcal T_s)\text{ is not continuous}.}
$$

If the direction were reversed, then the identity map $(\mathbb R,\mathcal T_s)\to(\mathbb R,\mathcal T_t)$ would be continuous.

### 12.
Any subspace $Y$ of a connected topological space $X$ is also connected.

### Solution

This statement is **false**.

Take $X=\mathbb R$, which is connected, and let
$$
Y=\{0,1\}\subseteq\mathbb R.
$$
In the subspace topology, $Y$ is discrete, because
$$
\{0\}=Y\cap(-1/2,1/2),\qquad \{1\}=Y\cap(1/2,3/2)
$$
are both open in $Y$.

Thus $Y$ is separated into two nonempty disjoint open sets, so $Y$ is disconnected.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{A subspace of a connected space need not be connected.}}
$$

### 13.
Any subspace $Y$ of a locally compact topological space $X$ is locally compact in the subspace topology.

### Solution

This statement is **false** in general.

A standard counterexample is
$$
Y=\mathbb Q\subseteq\mathbb R=X.
$$
The space $\mathbb R$ is locally compact, but $\mathbb Q$ is not locally compact in the subspace topology.

To see this, let $q\in\mathbb Q$ and let $U$ be any neighborhood of $q$ in $\mathbb Q$. Then there exists $\varepsilon>0$ such that
$$
(q-\varepsilon,q+\varepsilon)\cap\mathbb Q\subseteq U.
$$
If $\mathbb Q$ were locally compact, some neighborhood of $q$ would have compact closure in $\mathbb Q$. But any such set contains a rational interval, and its closure in $\mathbb Q$ contains points of $\mathbb Q$ converging to an irrational limit. That closure is therefore not compact.

So $\mathbb Q$ is not locally compact.

Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{A subspace of a locally compact space need not be locally compact}.}
$$

### 14.
Suppose $f_i:X_i\to Y_i$ for $i=1,2$ are covering maps. Then the map
$$
F:X_1\times X_2\to Y_1\times Y_2,
\qquad
F(x_1,x_2)=(f_1(x_1),f_2(x_2))
$$
is necessarily a covering map.

### Solution

This statement is true.

Let $(y_1,y_2)\in Y_1\times Y_2$. Since $f_1$ and $f_2$ are covering maps, there exist evenly covered neighborhoods $U_1$ of $y_1$ and $U_2$ of $y_2$ such that
$$
f_1^{-1}(U_1)=\bigsqcup_{\alpha} V_{1,\alpha},
\qquad
f_2^{-1}(U_2)=\bigsqcup_{\beta} V_{2,\beta},
$$
where each restriction
$$
f_1:V_{1,\alpha}\to U_1,
\qquad
f_2:V_{2,\beta}\to U_2
$$
is a homeomorphism.

Then
$$
F^{-1}(U_1\times U_2)=f_1^{-1}(U_1)\times f_2^{-1}(U_2)
=\bigsqcup_{\alpha,\beta}(V_{1,\alpha}\times V_{2,\beta}).
$$

On each product piece $V_{1,\alpha}\times V_{2,\beta}$, the map $F$ restricts to
$$
F:V_{1,\alpha}\times V_{2,\beta}\to U_1\times U_2,
$$
and this restriction is the product of two homeomorphisms, hence itself a homeomorphism.

Therefore $U_1\times U_2$ is evenly covered by $F$, so $F$ is a covering map.

Thus
$$
\boxed{F:X_1\times X_2\to Y_1\times Y_2\text{ is a covering map}.}
$$

### 15.
Prove that the map
$$
M:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R,
\qquad
M(x_1,\dots,x_d)=\prod_{i=1}^d x_i
$$
is continuous. Hence show that every polynomial
$$
p(x)=\alpha_d x^d+\cdots+\alpha_1 x+\alpha_0
$$
with $\alpha_i\in\mathbb R$ is a continuous function of the real variable $x$.

### Solution

We prove continuity of the product map first.

The multiplication map
$$
m:\mathbb R\times\mathbb R\to\mathbb R,
\qquad
m(a,b)=ab
$$
is continuous. This is a standard fact from analysis: it follows, for example, because
$$
ab-a_0b_0=(a-a_0)b+(b-b_0)a_0
$$
and each term can be made small when $a\to a_0$ and $b\to b_0$.

Now define recursively
$$
M_2(x_1,x_2)=x_1x_2,
\qquad
M_k(x_1,\dots,x_k)=M_{k-1}(x_1,\dots,x_{k-1})x_k.
$$
Since a composition of continuous maps is continuous, each $M_k$ is continuous. In particular, $M_d=M$ is continuous.

So
$$
\boxed{M:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R\text{ is continuous}.}
$$

Now let
$$
p(x)=\alpha_d x^d+\cdots+\alpha_1x+\alpha_0.
$$
Each map $x\mapsto x^k$ is continuous because it is the $k$-fold product of $x$ with itself. Multiplication by a constant $\alpha_k$ is continuous, and sums of continuous functions are continuous.

Therefore every polynomial function $p:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ is continuous.

Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{every real polynomial function is continuous}.}
$$

### 16.
Let
$$
X=\{0\}\cup\left\{n+\frac1n : n\ge 3\right\}
$$
and let
$$
f:\mathbb R\to S^1\subseteq\mathbb C,
\qquad
f(x)=e^{2\pi i x}.
$$
Prove that the restriction $f|_X:X\to f(X)$ is a bijective continuous map between locally compact topological spaces, but $f$ is not a homeomorphism.

### Solution

First note that $X$ is a subspace of $\mathbb R$ consisting of isolated points.

Indeed, $0$ is isolated because
$$
X\cap(-1/2,1/2)=\{0\},
$$
and each point $n+\frac1n$ is isolated because the numbers $n+\frac1n$ are separated from each other and tend to infinity, so each has a small interval in $\mathbb R$ meeting $X$ in only that point.

Thus $X$ is discrete, hence locally compact.

Now compute the image:
$$
f(0)=1,
\qquad
f\left(n+\frac1n\right)=e^{2\pi i(n+1/n)}=e^{2\pi i/n}.
$$
So
$$
f(X)=\{1\}\cup\left\{e^{2\pi i/n}:n\ge 3\right\}.
$$
The restriction $f|_X$ is clearly injective because distinct points of $X$ map to distinct points of $f(X)$, and it is surjective by definition of $f(X)$.

Hence $f|_X:X\to f(X)$ is a bijection. It is continuous because it is the restriction of the continuous map $f$.

The space $f(X)$ is locally compact as a subspace of the compact Hausdorff space $S^1$; in fact $f(X)$ is compact because it contains the limit point $1$ of the sequence $e^{2\pi i/n}$.

However, $f|_X$ is not a homeomorphism because its inverse is not continuous. Indeed,
$$
e^{2\pi i/n}\to 1\quad\text{in }f(X),
$$
but the preimages are
$$
n+\frac1n\to\infty
$$
in $\mathbb R$, and in the subspace $X$ they do not converge to $0$ (or to any point of $X$). In particular, the inverse map cannot send the convergent sequence $e^{2\pi i/n}\to 1$ to a convergent sequence in $X$.

So $f|_X$ is a continuous bijection between locally compact spaces, but it is not a homeomorphism.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{f|_X\text{ is continuous and bijective, but not a homeomorphism}.}
$$

### 17.
Define a $T_i$ space for $0\le i\le 6$. Give one example of a topological space that is $T_i$ for every $0\le i\le 6$.

### Solution

There are several equivalent naming conventions in the literature; the standard hierarchy is as follows.

### The separation axioms

1. A space is $T_0$ if for any two distinct points, at least one of them has an open neighborhood not containing the other.
2. A space is $T_1$ if for any two distinct points, each has a neighborhood not containing the other. Equivalently, every singleton is closed.
3. A space is $T_2$ if it is Hausdorff: any two distinct points have disjoint neighborhoods.
4. A space is $T_3$ if it is regular and $T_1$: given a point and a disjoint closed set, there are disjoint open neighborhoods separating them.
5. A space is $T_4$ if it is normal and $T_1$: any two disjoint closed sets have disjoint open neighborhoods.
6. A space is $T_5$ if it is completely normal and $T_1$.
7. A space is $T_6$ if it is perfectly normal.

### Example

Any discrete topological space is $T_i$ for every $0\le i\le 6$.

Indeed, in a discrete space every subset is open and closed, so all separation properties hold automatically.

Thus one example is
$$
\boxed{\text{any discrete topological space}.}
$$

### 18.
State and prove the Tietze extension theorem.

### Solution

### Statement

Let $X$ be a normal topological space and let $A\subseteq X$ be closed. Then every continuous function
$$
f:A\to[-1,1]
$$
extends to a continuous function
$$
F:X\to[-1,1]
$$
such that $F|_A=f$.

Equivalently, if $f:A\to\mathbb R$ is continuous and bounded, then $f$ extends to a continuous function on $X$.

### Proof idea

The proof is constructive and uses Urysohn's lemma repeatedly.

First reduce to the case $f:A\to[-1,1]$ by scaling and translating a bounded real-valued map.

The strategy is to build continuous functions on $X$ that approximate $f$ on $A$ more and more accurately, with errors $1/2,1/4,1/8,\dots$.

### Step 1: Separate upper and lower level sets

For a continuous $f:A\to[-1,1]$, consider the closed subsets of $A$
$$
A^- = f^{-1}\left([-1,-\tfrac13]\right),
\qquad
A^+ = f^{-1}\left([\tfrac13,1]\right).
$$
These are closed in $A$, hence of the form $A\cap C^-$ and $A\cap C^+$ for closed sets in $X$.
Because $X$ is normal, Urysohn's lemma gives a continuous function $u_1:X\to[-1/3,1/3]$ that is $-1/3$ on $A^-$ and $1/3$ on $A^+$.

Subtracting this first approximation from $f$ leaves a new continuous function on $A$ whose range is smaller. Repeating the procedure with finer and finer bounds yields a uniformly convergent series of continuous functions on $X$.

### Step 2: Uniform convergence

One constructs continuous functions $u_n:X\to\mathbb R$ such that
$$
\|u_n\|_\infty\le 2^{-n}
$$
and the partial sums
$$
F_N=u_1+u_2+\cdots+u_N
$$
converge uniformly on $A$ to $f$.

Since the $u_n$ are continuous and the series converges uniformly, the sum
$$
F(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty u_n(x)
$$
is continuous on $X$.

By construction, $F|_A=f$ and $F(X)\subseteq[-1,1]$.

Thus every continuous function on a closed subset of a normal space extends to the whole space.

So the Tietze extension theorem holds:
$$
\boxed{\text{If }X\text{ is normal and }A\subseteq X\text{ is closed, every }f:A\to[-1,1]\text{ extends to }X.}
$$

### 19.
Consider the topological space $X=\mathbb R^d\setminus\{0\}$ and the action of $\mathbb R^*=\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$ on $X$ given by
$$
\lambda\cdot x=\lambda(x_1,\dots,x_d)=(\lambda x_1,\dots,\lambda x_d).
$$
Define a relation on $X$ by $x\sim y$ if and only if there exists $\lambda\in\mathbb R^*$ such that $\lambda\cdot x=y$.

(a) Prove that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation.

(b) Prove that $X/\sim$ is compact and path connected in the quotient topology.

### Solution

#### (a) $\sim$ is an equivalence relation

We verify the three properties.

Reflexive: for every $x\in X$, take $\lambda=1$. Then $1\cdot x=x$, so $x\sim x$.

Symmetric: if $x\sim y$, then $y=\lambda x$ for some $\lambda\in\mathbb R^*$. Since $\lambda\ne 0$, we also have $x=\lambda^{-1}y$, so $y\sim x$.

Transitive: if $x\sim y$ and $y\sim z$, then $y=\lambda x$ and $z=\mu y$ for some nonzero reals $\lambda,\mu$. Hence
$$
z=(\mu\lambda)x,
$$
so $x\sim z$.

Therefore $\sim$ is an equivalence relation.

#### (b) Compactness and path connectedness of the quotient

Each equivalence class is a line through the origin with the origin removed. Thus the quotient space is naturally the real projective space
$$
X/\sim\;\cong\;\mathbb RP^{d-1}.
$$

To see compactness, observe that every equivalence class meets the unit sphere
$$
S^{d-1}=\{x\in\mathbb R^d:\|x\|=1\}.
$$
Indeed, if $x\ne 0$, then $x/\|x\|\in S^{d-1}$ is equivalent to $x$. The quotient map
$$
S^{d-1}\to X/\sim
$$
is continuous and surjective. Since $S^{d-1}$ is compact, its continuous image is compact. Therefore $X/\sim$ is compact.

For path connectedness, consider two equivalence classes represented by $u,v\in S^{d-1}$.

If $d=1$, then $X=\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$ has only one equivalence class under scaling, so the quotient is a single point and hence path connected.

If $d\ge 2$, the sphere $S^{d-1}$ is path connected. Choose a path in $S^{d-1}$ joining $u$ to either $v$ or $-v$; its projection to the quotient gives a path from $[u]$ to $[v]$.

Hence $X/\sim$ is path connected.

Therefore
$$
\boxed{X/\sim\text{ is compact and path connected}.}
$$

### 20.
Let $I=[0,1]\subset\mathbb R$. Let $f:X\to Y$ be a covering map where $X$ and $Y$ are topological spaces. Let
$$
G:I\times I\to Y
$$
be a continuous map. Suppose $G(0,0)=y_0$ and $x_0\in f^{-1}(y_0)$. Prove that there exists a unique map
$$
g:I\times I\to X
$$
such that $g(0,0)=x_0$ and $f\circ g=G$.

### Solution

This is the standard lifting theorem for coverings applied to the square $I\times I$.

The key facts are:

- $I\times I$ is path connected.
- $I\times I$ is simply connected.

Because $f:X\to Y$ is a covering map, every path in $Y$ starting at $y_0$ has a unique lift starting at $x_0$.

### Existence

For each point $u\in I\times I$, choose any path $\gamma_u$ in $I\times I$ from $(0,0)$ to $u$. Then $G\circ\gamma_u$ is a path in $Y$ starting at $y_0$.

Lift this path uniquely to a path $\widetilde\gamma_u$ in $X$ starting at $x_0$. Define
$$
g(u)=\widetilde\gamma_u(1).
$$

Because $I\times I$ is simply connected, any two paths from $(0,0)$ to $u$ are homotopic rel endpoints. The homotopy lifting property for coverings implies that their lifted endpoints agree. Hence $g(u)$ is well defined.

### Continuity

Continuity follows from local triviality of the covering map: around each point of $Y$, there is an evenly covered neighborhood, and the lift varies continuously over such neighborhoods. This is the standard local lifting argument.

### Uniqueness

If $g_1$ and $g_2$ are two lifts with $g_1(0,0)=g_2(0,0)=x_0$, then for each point $u$ the paths $g_1\circ\gamma_u$ and $g_2\circ\gamma_u$ are lifts of the same path $G\circ\gamma_u$ starting at the same point. By uniqueness of path lifting, they coincide. Hence $g_1=g_2$.

Therefore there exists a unique lift $g$ such that
$$
\boxed{g(0,0)=x_0\quad\text{and}\quad f\circ g=G.}
$$

## Part D

### 21.
Let $X$ be a locally compact Hausdorff space, $Y$ a normal space and $f:X\to Y$ be a covering map such that $f^{-1}(y)$ is a finite set for every $y\in Y$. Prove that every pair $(K,g_K)$ determines a continuous function $G:X\to[0,1]$ such that $G|_K=g_K$, where $K\subseteq X$ is closed and $g_K:K\to[0,1]$ is continuous. What is the significance of the hypothesis that $f^{-1}(y)$ is finite for every $y\in Y$?

### Solution

The intended conclusion is a Tietze-extension-type statement for $X$.

The key point is that a covering map with finite fibers is a **finite-sheeted covering**. For locally compact Hausdorff spaces, finite-sheeted coverings are proper and hence closed maps. This is the role of the finiteness assumption: it forces the covering to behave like a proper map rather than an infinite-sheeted one.

Once the map is closed and the target space $Y$ is normal, the standard perfect-map theorem applies: the source space $X$ inherits normality from $Y$.

After that, the Tietze extension theorem applies to $X$: if $K\subseteq X$ is closed and $g_K:K\to[0,1]$ is continuous, then there exists a continuous extension
$$
G:X\to[0,1]
$$
with
$$
G|_K=g_K.
$$

So the conclusion is
$$
\boxed{\text{every continuous }g_K:K\to[0,1]\text{ extends to }G:X\to[0,1].}
$$

The significance of the finiteness hypothesis is that it ensures the covering has finitely many sheets over each point, which is what makes the covering map closed/proper and allows normality to be transferred to $X$.
